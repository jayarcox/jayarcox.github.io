The Boy and the Fire

by
J.R. Cox

A boy and man were travelling together. As the sun began to get low, the man stopped in a clearing. The man said to the boy, “I will start a fire, and go down to the river to fish.”

The man started the fire, and it was very small and puny. The man then said to the boy, “This fire is week now, but it wants to be strong. Watch that it does not grow and do not feed it. I will come back with fish soon.”

Then the man left, and the boy sat down next to the fire. It was hard for the boy to sit and watch the fire while the man was fishing. The boy wanted to go to the river too, but he did as the man said.

Finally there was a great wind, and the fire waned. The boy became frightened that the fire would go out. If the fire went out, then the boy would have no job to do. Surely the man did not want the boy to let the fire die. So he fed the fire just a little bit to keep it going.

The fire ate the bark the boy threw on it very fast, but it did look a little better. The boy felt proud of himself for making the fire strong enough to withstand the wind. A mighty gust came again, and this time the fire did not wane. The boy was very proud.

As the shrill sound of the wind fell, the boy heard a little hiss come from the fire. He thought it might have been a whisper, but he thought it was more likely a bit of smoke escaping the wood. It was getting colder though, and the sun was going down, so he moved a bit closer to the fire. He heard the little hiss again, and this time he thought it very well could have been a whisper. So he knelt down next to the weak little fire, and he felt the satisfying warmth on his face.

Sure enough the fire hissed again, “I’m starving. I’m starving.” The boy was surprised. The fire did still look quite puny. He felt bad for the little fire, but he knew what the man had said. “I’m sorry,” said the boy, “I can’t feed you.”

The wind blew again, and this time the fire waned a bit, and it whispered again, “I’m starving, I’m starving.”

The boy thought about how he had kept the fire from dying. “I suppose if you really are starving,” said the boy, and he threw a bit more bark on the fire and it grew a little bigger.

“Thank you,” said the fire, “I’m starving. I’m starving.”

“I just fed you,” said the boy, “and I shouldn’t have done that.”

The wind blew again, and now the sun was very near the treetops. The boy was very cold. The boy thought about it. He knew he was not supposed to feed it, but the man would already be angry that the fire had grown, so he might as well be warm if he were going to be in trouble. He threw some wood on the fire. The sun dipped behind the trees, and the boy knew the man would be back from the river soon. He heard footsteps in the forest, and when he looked, he saw many shining eyes looking at the fire.

“Oh no,” said the boy, “You’ve gotten too big now, and the wolves and the beasts have seen us, and they will eat me now.”

“I’m starving,” said the fire.

Now the boy realized why he should have kept the fire small. He knew the man would be very angry that he had attracted the wolves, so he quickly came up with a plan. Maybe, if he could scare them off, the man would be a little easier on him.

So the boy threw the rest of the wood on the fire and it grew large and roared at the wolves, and the wolves ran away. The boy was proud again, but now the fire was very, very large. So large that it began to lick at the treetops that knelt in toward the clearing.

“I’m starving,” said the fire as it began to spread to the forest outside of the clearing.

“But how can you still be hungry?” asked the boy, “I have already given you all the firewood.”

The fire kept eating away at the forest, so the boy ran out into the woods and he chased the animals out into the clearing, and the fire ate them up greedily, but it only grew larger and continued to nibble at the forest.

“Stop fire!” shouted the boy, “If you eat the forest the man will be so angry he’ll feed me to you.”

The fire raged, “I’m starving! I’m starving!”

The boy wasn’t sure now if the fire would eat him anyway. He wished he had never fed the fire. He felt so guilty that he had disobeyed the man, but he did not want to be consumed by the fire. The boy ran into the forest and found the man, who was already rushing to the smoke.

“What have you done?” asked the man, “I told you not to feed the fire, and to keep it weak.”

The boy was too frightened and ashamed to say anything. He just grabbed the man’s hand and took him close to the fire and the fire gobbled up the man. “I’m starving,” cackled the fire, “I’m starving,” and it feasted on all the trees and plants in the forest.

The boy looked around and saw that there was nothing left to feed the fire. “Fire,” he said, “You have eaten the land, and you have eaten the trees and you have eaten the animals, and you have even eaten the man. There is nothing else I can bring you to eat. If you are still starving, then you will always be starving.”

Then the fire reached down, and the boy thought it might apologize. Instead, it snatched up the boy and ate him too, and all it would say is, “I’m starving, I’m starving.”
