The Republic of the Great Pacific Garbage Patch

by
J.R. Cox

Waded was Jonathon’s first wife. Despite that, it didn’t seem strange at all that there were no rumors. No gossip about what had become of her. Few of us knew her at all and those that did, didn’t know her well. I can’t speak for all of us, but I certainly felt her departure from the Republic was proof that I was there of my own volition. After all, even his first wife left freely. I didn’t bother examining it much deeper than that. If her disappearance was anything other than her own voluntary cessation from our new state, then what would that say about me and my choices? My freedom? My beliefs? I didn’t bother asking those questions then. The most reasonable explanation was also the least frightening. If you think about it logically like that, you start to realize a little bit of how we wound up there. Everyone takes some amount of their reality on faith. You have to. At least some.

I think the least generous description of our nation was from an article that read, “The so-called ‘state’ that the Patchers founded is more accurately pictured as a shipwreck orgy that may have been terrifying if it had even amounted to the level of society in ‘The Lord of the Flies’.” I don’t even disagree with most of it, but what I think gets lost in the ridicule is that we managed to create a place to live that wasn’t terrifying. Even when Tiffany, Jonathan's new first wife vanished, the predominant feeling was one of despair. I’m not attempting to defend myself. Despair isn’t necessarily better, but I defy you to find a real country that isn’t, under scrutiny, terrifying.

That’s one reason I can’t really blame Jonathon. He manipulated us. He lied to us. It was a cult. I am willing to admit that, but I don’t feel like he took advantage of me. Part of me was always aware that what I was doing was, objectively, ridiculous. I was just sick of being terrified. To say that Jonathon brainwashed me would really be giving him too much credit.

I was an optimist. Maybe I still am. First of all, he looked like the comic book guy from the Simpsons. The first thing he talked to me about was how awesome he was at Tony Hawk Pro Skater 2, and he’d probably still be awesome if his friend would ever admit to stealing it and give it back to him. I asked him why he didn’t just buy a new copy and he told me he already owned it so why would he buy it again. That was all he said to me that day. We were at a democratic rally. I thought maybe he got lost until I saw him at the primaries.

Gretch, his first wife at the time, was talking to me about the Individualist’s Manifesto which I had recently read. I’m not proud to say that I was frantically trying to steer the conversation into a romantic area without disrespecting her femininity, which I knew even then was a contradiction in terms. My enthusiasm for the manifesto was the only reason I persisted when she told me she wanted to introduce me to her husband.

My surprise that Jonathon had landed such an attractive and young wife was more persuasive than anything he said to me at the primary, but we did wind up having a lot in common ideologically. I wasn’t the only waifish teenager that happily signed up to receive his newsletter. Gretch continued to mingle and recruit a particular profile throughout the event.

It’s funny to realize how hard I worked, how much will power and mental determination it took to keep myself involved in the group at first. I had convinced myself it was our civic duty to be activists. My apathy and malaise probably would have won out if Jonathon’s radical interpretation of a radical political text hadn’t so quickly become my main means of getting laid. At some point, the practical benefits started supporting what I knew deep down were irrational ideas. That’s the line I crossed from being an impressionable, obedient cog in America’s political machine to an impressionable, obedient acolyte of Jonathon’s.
Since most people haven’t read it, the Individualist Manifesto contained a section of thought experiments, one of which was a sovereign nation built out of the Great Pacific Garbage Patch. The author, whom I have come to suspect was not Jonathon after all, suggests that a nation built anew, without the historical sins of other governments would be the most efficient means to global Utopia. Unfortunately, there is no practical way of implementing that concept on already populated or claimed land, so, maybe even as a joke, they posed the Garbage Patch as the most tenable option of putting the thought experiment into practice.

Having read that, and discussed the theory at length, it didn’t seem like a stretch when Jonathon said we were doing it. There was a bit of a schism on the message board, but he told us that was natural, even expected, in the formation of new governments. That much was true.

We were meeting up pretty often after that, and things moved quickly; although, not as quickly as it seemed because we were imbibing pretty heavily in edible marijuana. The remainder of the Patchers pooled our savings and to my surprise the yacht that Jonathon bought with it was pretty incredible. I imagine he got a great deal because I doubt many of us had much money. There certainly wasn’t much left over which is what led to the ill-fated press conference from which the popular nickname “garbage patch kids’ was born. Though we didn’t get as many donations as we had hoped, or even planned for, there was enough to afford what we thought would be our basic needs: condoms, lube, marijuana seeds, chickens, rice, etc. Unfortunately the list didn’t include items like soil, fertilizer, chicken feed, potable water, or any sort of survival literature. I was stupid to trust Jonathon had everything under control, but my parents shoulder some of the responsibility for my lack of preparedness. I wasn’t just unfit to go on an indefinite sea voyage; I wasn’t even fit to live outside of their house unassisted for more than a weekend.
The most cynical thing about this whole embarrassing affair is that, despite lacking any knowledge of navigation or seamanship, the Garbage Patch was easy for us to find. We realized pretty quickly when the chickens died and no one, not even Jonathon’s contemporary first wife, Bronwyn, knew how to safely prepare them for consumption, that we hadn’t planned very well. Anything remotely edible in the Garbage Patch had been ingested by fish or algae or turtles long ago and the grand hillsides of recyclable waste that we had imagined were nowhere to be found. Just an endless sprawl of loosely amalgamated, slimy straws and toilet lids from horizon to horizon.

I was heartened by Jonathon’s indefatigable spirit, and was thrilled by his plan of collecting bottles and lashing them into makeshift pontoon islands. Already the warped sense of manifest destiny had taken hold in my brain and I cheerfully began searching the flotilla for detritus that could be cut or twisted into string. I was even blind to the cracks beginning to form in the loyalty of my comrades.

Jonathan told us that one day the history books would lawd our toughness of constitution for eating dry, uncooked rice to survive during the birth of our metropolis. Or at least I think that’s what he was getting at. By that time we had discarded the use of English for its inherent patriarchal and racist phonemes. However, I must admit I was not fluent enough to understand that Waded was leaving Jonathon for good when she gave her speech from a trash bin she had formed into a canoe, so my impressions of what was said after that point are a dubious account at best. Come to think of it, that might be why there was so little gossip over her departure.

I want to say it was a few days later that people started asking after Tiffany, but I wasn’t the only one struggling with the new language. For a day or two I assumed she was just on the other side of the boat, and people on the other side of the boat assumed she was with us. After maybe five days of this, it seemed we all felt comfortable with the theory that she had gone off to join Waded. Again, that’s just my best guess because the combination of the new language and magic mushrooms didn’t lend itself to accurate communication.

It came as somewhat of a relief when Waded returned a week after that speaking English, but the feeling was also marred by her declaration of invasion. She and her male harem had formed the Federation of Artificial Islands, and were taking the Yacht by force. At what point her masculine escort had left us and joined her is anyone’s guess. Of course Jonathon would never surrender, but all of us were so malnourished at that point that not even the orgy that followed the fight included much bodily fluids. When most of us had passed out from exhaustion, Jonathan called for an emergency election and lost his presidency to Waded.
Waded in her first act of leadership radioed the coast guard to alert them of the new political status of the FAI. They responded by asking if we needed help, and that was the first time I realized that we desperately, desperately did. Waded said no, but would like to set up a diplomatic relationship with the USA. She was admonished for misusing the frequency.

They tell me that it was day 17 of our grand experiment that the weather got bad. I would have estimated it was months. Upon listening to the news reports after returning, I learned of a weather phenomenon colloquially called El Nino. Apparently every few years there is a predictable, but heavily destructive weather pattern that ravages the pacific. Now I find it more surprising that we survived to see it than the fact that we survived through it. Well, all of us except Tiffany.

Supposedly, Waded’s near constant requests for contact with world leadership and the press bore some fruit in that a reporter had caught wind and was planning on covering our disappearance. She came out early to beat the worst of the storm and was mildly disappointed to find us still afloat.

Jonathan was at that point all too happy to wash his hands of it, but Waded had to be forced onto the rescue boat. Some of the most zealous of us gave half-hearted protestations, but most of us were too tired and jaded to do anything but what the red cross told us. I heard that Jonathon and his latest first wife Persia are still trying to claim refugee status, but I mostly keep my distance from that circle for obvious reasons. While it is profoundly embarrassing to recall, there is also a small part of me that can’t help taking a tiny bit of pride in our bravery. Most people will go there whole lives without doing anything so indefensibly stupid, but the reason for that isn’t cleverness. It’s fear.

Sometimes I wonder what would have happened if anyone in our group was even remotely competent. The easy answer is that they would have stopped us from doing anything at all, and I suppose that’s what makes changing the status quo so hard, isn’t it?
